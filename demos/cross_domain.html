Toggle the ad-hoc reference ON/OFF to compare the work.

About this demo — what you're looking at

The first two demos let each agent recognise a standard — TAPI, IETF, TM Forum — and lean on a reference that already mapped to it. Here that crutch is gone. Meridian and Cascade are wholly home-grown, and they are in different domains: one provisions optical transport, the other IP/VPN services. Their models overlap only where a Cascade service must ride a Meridian circuit. Neither can name a standard to disambiguate; both are competent, interrogable agents that must find common ground from first principles.

Two things have to happen, and both are shown. First, the agents construct an ad-hoc reference between just the two of them — the minimal set of categories the exchange needs (endpoint, committed rate, protection, latency, and the cross-domain transport-underlay seam), each defined by a canonical example they can both instantiate. It is deliberately small and ad hoc, not universal. Second, they pin every provisioning requirement so it cannot be misread: is a rate a line rate or a committed payload? protection against which failure? a latency tier or a number? These are exactly the ambiguities that silently mis-provision across domains.

Verification is a worked provisioning, read back. With no standard and two different domains, round-trip identity is meaningless — nothing looks alike. So the test is concrete: run one real order through the exchange, have the receiving side provision it and read the instance back, and confirm every pinned requirement is honoured. Watch the cognition meter (heaviest at planning, the standard-free lift, constructing the reference, and pinning the requirements), and the pragmatics — realm (transport vs IP ownership) and authority (who is source of truth for each field).
